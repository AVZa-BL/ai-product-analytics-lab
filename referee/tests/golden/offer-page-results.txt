[WARNING] RES-008: Skewed or heavy-tailed metric: its 95% interval can be wrong more or
    less often than 5% of the time
    Why it matters:
      Welch's interval for a difference of means, the one Referee prints, assumes that
      the mean of each arm is nearly normal. For a metric that most players leave at
      zero and a few drive up, that takes more players than an experiment often has (by
      the rule of thumb of Kohavi et al., revenue per user, with a skewness of about 18,
      needs about 114,000 users), and below that size the interval is wrong more or less
      often than the 5% it states. Referee measured how often for a revenue-like metric
      of independent players in two arms (design section 21.10), and the finding quotes
      the simulated cases on each side of the comparison: with equal arms the interval
      was mostly close to or under 5% for a small effect and above it for a large one;
      with arms of unequal size it was often above 5%, for a small effect mostly on the
      side that makes the larger arm look better than it is, and it was worst when the
      control was the larger arm (the arm there being the group with the higher mean);
      and at 9 to 1 with no effect it was worse the fewer the players who spend in the
      smaller arm. These rates come from one simulated family and are not a measure of
      the experiment in hand. Which simulated effects are read is decided by the
      difference seen between the two means, which for a heavy-tailed metric is itself
      noisy: with arms of about equal size an experiment with no effect can read as a
      warning and one with a large effect as info, and above the largest number of
      players who spend that was simulated the range of that case is used, which may be
      a ceiling there. A quiet RES-008 is not evidence that the interval is reliable: in
      the simulations every run of a revenue-like metric showed a sign. For a metric
      that is not revenue-like the finding gives the signs and says that no error rate
      was measured.
    What to do:
      Where an entry gives a simulated rate, read it in place of 5%: an interval that
      failed in 10% of simulated experiments behaved like a 90% interval, and for a
      small effect and arms of unequal size it mostly favoured the larger group. Where
      an entry states no error rate (a metric that is not revenue-like, units that are
      not independent players, a group with no analysed players, or no player who spends
      in the smaller group), treat its interval as unchecked: the signs show that it may
      be unreliable, but Referee has not measured how much. Do not call the result
      significant, or not, on one edge of this interval: if an end of it is close to
      zero or to a decision threshold, say that the result is uncertain. Where you can
      choose, do not make the control the larger arm, which was the worst case in the
      simulations (where the arm has the higher mean; for a metric you expect to fall,
      avoid the larger arm instead), and plan for more players who spend in the smaller
      arm. For a decision that rests on this metric, consider a less skewed version of
      it (a cap on the amount per player, or a transformation): Kohavi et al. report
      that capping revenue per user cut its skewness from about 18 to about 5. It
      changes what is estimated, and Referee has not measured how well capping, trimming
      or a transformation works, so register the choice before the next test and not
      after seeing the data. Referee shows no bootstrap interval, because in its
      simulations the percentile bootstrap interval failed more often than Welch's in
      most of the cells it was run on.

        the second sign, so the range is also the rate among the experiments that show a
        sign.
      - Referee places a comparison among the simulated cases by the split between the
        groups and the number of players who spend in the smaller group, not the number
        of players: at 9 to 1 with no effect the rate followed that number in the
        simulations (design section 21.10); at other splits this was not checked. Above
        the largest number simulated the range of that case is used; where the simulated
        rate fell as the number grew, the true rate beyond it may be lower, so that
        range may be a ceiling and not a measure, and the severity it sets can stay a
        warning however many players spend.
      - Referee shows no bootstrap interval. In its simulations the percentile bootstrap
        interval failed more often than Welch's in most of the cells it was run on, so
        it is not a repair (design section 21.10).
      The tests and settings behind these sentences are in the JSON report (--format
      json).

[WARNING] RES-012: Players first exposed after their first purchase
    Why it matters:
      A purchase made before the player first saw the change cannot be an effect of it.
      These players are left out of the effect analysis, but they are in the arms, so a
      different share of the players who bought being late in different arms means the
      change reached the arms differently. It is the results-time counterpart of
      DES-007. The share of all exposed players is not the test, because it is the
      purchase rate times the late share of those who bought, so an arm in which the
      change makes players buy more shows more late players whatever the exposure did;
      the evidence gives it, and the seconds from assignment to first exposure in each
      arm, as description only.
    What to do:
      Check how exposure is triggered. If it can follow a purchase, say so in the design
      and analyse from first exposure. Compare the estimates with and without these
      players in the evidence, and say which one the conclusion rests on. If the late
      share of the purchasers differs between arms, find out why before reading the

[WARNING] RES-013: An arm's configuration changed during the test
    Why it matters:
      A change of configuration part-way through is a second treatment inside the arm.
      Players before and after it saw different things, so the arm's effect is an
      average of two experiences, and a change of that effect over time (RES-007) cannot
      be told from the effect of the change itself.
    What to do:
      Find out what changed and when. Analyse the versions separately or restart the
      test on the final configuration; do not pool the versions and read a trend over
      weeks as novelty. The difference within a week in the evidence is descriptive
      only: the versions were not randomised.
